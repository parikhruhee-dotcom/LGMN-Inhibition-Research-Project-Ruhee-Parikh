# Repurposing screen: approved drugs against legumain

Generated by `src/repurposing.py`.

Every other part of this project evaluates compounds already known to inhibit
legumain. This asks whether anything **new** turns up, and looks among approved
drugs because a hit there is immediately testable: the compound can be bought, and
its safety and dosing are already established.

> **Nothing here is a hit.** These are compounds that survive filtering and score
> plausibly. A model with negative R2 on unseen chemistry cannot identify an
> inhibitor; it can only say which compounds are worth an experiment first.

### How to read this

These are starting points for an experiment, not predictions of activity. The
model behind the potency column cannot predict absolute potency for chemistry
outside its training set, and approved drugs are by definition outside it. The
similarity column is the honest part: the closer to 1.0, the more the model has
actually seen something like this compound.

### Why the list is this short, and what the survivor means

Over a thousand approved drugs are predicted to reach the brain and carry no
covalent warhead. Almost none of them resemble a legumain inhibitor closely
enough for this model to say anything about. That is the project's central
finding arriving from the opposite direction: the chemistry that crosses into the
brain and the chemistry that blocks this enzyme barely overlap, and the approved
pharmacopoeia contains essentially none of the intersection.

The compound that does survive is instructive, and not in a flattering way.
**Caffeine** clears the filters because the legumain dataset contains a series of
*thio*-xanthines, and its nearest neighbour (CHEMBL4092935, pIC50 6.74) is caffeine
with one sulfur atom added at position 8. The model sees two nearly identical
structures and predicts nearly identical potency.

That single atom is very likely the point. A thione sits where a plain carbonyl
cannot, and in a cysteine protease the sulfur is the obvious candidate for the
interaction that matters. A model built on fingerprints has no way to know which
atom carries the pharmacophore; it only knows the two molecules look alike. The
most probable truth is that caffeine does not inhibit legumain at any useful
concentration, and that this prediction is the applicability domain failing at its
own boundary -- a similarity of 0.47 is only just inside it.

It is worth stating plainly because the test is trivial. Caffeine is in every
laboratory, and the enzyme assay already exists. Someone could settle this in an
afternoon, and the expected answer is no.

A search of PubMed found no published report of caffeine or related xanthines
inhibiting legumain, so this is not a rediscovery of something known.

## What this does not do

- It does not test binding. No compound here has been docked unless noted, and
  docking in this project carries weak signal at best (`docs/DOCKING.md`).
- It does not consider whether a drug's existing targets would matter. A compound
  that inhibits legumain and ten other enzymes is not a useful tool.
- It does not consider dose. Reaching the brain at all is different from reaching it
  at a concentration that inhibits anything.
